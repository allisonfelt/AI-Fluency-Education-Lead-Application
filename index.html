01 · The program

I built it. Then I checked if it worked.

At Apple Education I kept hearing smart people explain the same product three different ways in one week. Not because they didn't care. Because nobody had ever sat down and taught them the thing they were being asked to explain.

So I did. Elevate is a four-week program I pitched, designed, and shipped — the curriculum, the order, and all the unglamorous decisions about what to cut. It answered one question: could someone explain this correctly a month later, with me nowhere in the building?

Turns out yes. 24% average knowledge lift. It won our 2025 Impact Award out of 500-odd nominations, which was lovely, but the number is the part that matters. You wrote that completion isn't comprehension. Agreed — which is why I measured before anyone asked me to.

02 · On simplifying

Simple isn’t shorter

“Simplify” usually gets used to mean “make it shorter,” and that isn’t it. Cutting words is easy. Working out what someone actually needs in order to make their own call is a different job, and it’s the whole job.

You mention nurses, teachers, small business owners, policymakers. I’ve spent three years on one of those groups — at Apple I own how we talk about Mac and iPad in education, which mostly means explaining something technical to a district administrator who has forty minutes and no interest in a spec sheet. They don’t need to know how the silicon works. They need just enough of a model to tell whether a claim in a vendor deck is plausible.

This isn't abstract for me. Before business school I spent a year in China, a year in Australia, and a stretch in Taiwan — illiterate each time — and then came home to Reading Partners in Oakland as their marketing and communications specialist. A literacy nonprofit is a good school for this work: the subject is how people learn to read, the audiences are funders, volunteers, districts, and families, and none of them can be talked to the same way.

So the test is: can this person decide something without me now? The smallest true model that lets someone reason on their own — and it moves for every audience. Which is why your posting stopped me. You wrote that the point is people making their own judgements with AI rather than copying someone else’s.

03 · The pipeline

I was shortening idea-to-published before AI could help

Before Apple I ran creative production at Stanford's business school — YouTube and Stanford Business magazine, the two things the team actually bet on. Five producers, twenty freelancers, four million people on the other end.

The one I'd point you to is Class Takeaways. I concepted and initiated it. We only made long-form video at the time, and there was an audience segment our content simply wasn't reaching — so I argued for short-form, identified the agency, ran the partnership, and built the program: thirty five-minute lessons, one idea each, lifted out of GSB courses and rebuilt for people who were never going to enrol in them.

I didn't shoot every episode. I built the thing that made them possible, which is the part I'd argue you're actually hiring for.

I also took the magazine digital: I owned the redesign end to end, wrote and ran the RFP, chose the design agency, and managed them through it. The first digital edition came back 98% positive. The rest was throughput: fifteen to twenty finished pieces a month, video output tripled by changing how we found subjects rather than by hiring anyone, and open rates up 80% once I finally sat down and read the audience data. You want the distance from idea to published to keep shrinking. That was already the job.

06 · How I work

The aligning is the work

Every job I've had has been cross-functional, which is a polite way of saying I spend a lot of time in rooms where six teams want six different things and every one of them is right. At Apple that's Research, Finance, Enterprise, Sales, Enablement, and Product. At Apple Retail it was Brand, Industrial Design, and Legal, on launches where the packaging, the campaign, and the lawyers all had to land on the same answer on the same morning.

I used to treat that as the tax you pay before the real work starts. It isn't. At any real scale, the aligning is the work — the thing a customer eventually hears is just the residue of everyone agreeing.

I'd also like to think I'm a good person to build something with. I've managed producers, freelancers, and four early-career folks out of Apple Retail, and the measure I actually care about is whether people finish a project having learned something and still wanting to work with me.

07 · The gaps

What I don't have

What I'd point at instead is a pattern. Four times now I've walked into a place whose format had quietly stopped fitting its audience, and changed the format: long-form video to short, print to digital, a sales organisation with no curriculum to one with a measured program, and this year written news into a podcast nobody has to sit down to read. You wrote that most AI education still looks like an online course from 2015. That is the problem I keep being handed, and I keep taking it.

So here's my case: the rarest thing on your list isn't a credential. It's having made people fluent in something and being able to prove it. I can read the learning science. Knowing what survives contact with a room you're not in took ten years and a lot of things that didn't work. I'd back that over a credential, and I'd like the chance to show you why.
