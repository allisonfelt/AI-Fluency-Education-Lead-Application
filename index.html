The program

At Apple Education I kept watching capable people give inconsistent answers about products they had sold for years. The gap wasn't effort or motivation. It was that nobody had ever built them a way to understand the thing they were explaining.

So I built one. Elevate is a four-week enablement program I proposed, designed, and shipped — not a deck handed to a training team, but the curriculum, the sequencing, and the call on what to leave out. It ran against a real question: could people explain the product correctly a month later, without me in the room?

They could. Average knowledge lift of 24%, and the program earned the organization's 2025 Impact Award, selected from more than 500 candidates. I mention the measurement rather than the award because you wrote that completion is not comprehension and comprehension is not changed behavior. I agree, which is why I instrumented for it before I needed to defend it.

The pipeline

Before Apple I ran creative production at Stanford Graduate School of Business, where I was promoted to own the two channels the team invested in most: YouTube and Stanford Business magazine. Five producers, roughly twenty freelancers, a combined audience near four million.

The relevant part is throughput. We moved to fifteen to twenty finished pieces a month across web, video, email, print, and social; I tripled video production by changing how we sourced subjects rather than by hiring; email open rates went up 80% once I actually looked at the audience data; and we shipped the first digital edition of the magazine, which came back 98% positive. I also started a faculty video series nobody had asked for, because there was an audience segment we were not reaching.

You wrote that you want the distance from an idea to a published piece to keep getting shorter while quality goes up. That was my job for two years, before there was any AI to help with it.
